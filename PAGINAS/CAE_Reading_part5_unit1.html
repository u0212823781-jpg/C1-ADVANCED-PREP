03

Exam task — Unit 1: Mind and technology

You are going to read an article in which a journalist writes about boredom and technology. For questions 1–6, choose the answer (A, B, C or D) which you think fits best according to the text.

Text size

In defence of boredom

01

For most of my twenties, I regarded boredom as a kind of personal failure. An empty afternoon was something to be filled, preferably with something that could later be described as productive. Waiting rooms, train journeys and queues were simply gaps between more worthwhile activities, and my phone was the tool I used to seal them shut. It was only when a stubborn wrist injury forced me to give up my phone for a month – typing had become painful, and my doctor was unsympathetic – that I began to suspect I had got things the wrong way round.

1What does the writer suggest about her attitude to boredom in her twenties?

Look at how she describes waiting rooms and queues, and what her phone did to them.

Answer B

Evidence“Waiting rooms, train journeys and queues were simply gaps between more worthwhile activities, and my phone was the tool I used to seal them shut.”

WhyTo 'seal something shut' means to close it completely. She used her phone to close every empty moment, so for her, free time was something to get rid of.

Why not the others?

  • A She only started to question her attitude later, after the injury ('It was only when... I began to suspect'). In her twenties she did not think it was unhealthy.
  • C She felt that boredom was a 'personal failure', but she never says she felt guilty about her phone.
  • D Other people's opinions are not mentioned at all.
02

The first week was, frankly, miserable. I found myself reaching for a device that wasn't there, and the restlessness that followed was almost physical. Yet somewhere in the second week, something shifted. On a delayed train, with nothing to read and no one to message, my mind began to wander in a way it hadn't for years. I thought about a conversation I'd had with my sister, about a half-finished project at work, and, rather absurdly, about why pigeons nod their heads when they walk. None of it was urgent. All of it felt oddly refreshing.

2The writer describes her experience on the train in the second paragraph in order to

Compare the first sentence of the paragraph with the last one. What word marks the change?

Answer A

Evidence“The first week was, frankly, miserable. ... Yet somewhere in the second week, something shifted. ... All of it felt oddly refreshing.”

WhyThe paragraph moves from 'miserable' to 'refreshing'. The word 'Yet' marks the change. The train story is the example of that change.

Why not the others?

  • B Her mind wanders freely on the train. The paragraph is not about a problem with concentrating.
  • C She says her thoughts were not urgent, but she describes them as 'refreshing', which is positive.
  • D She never says she decided to make the experiment longer.
03

Psychologists have a name for what was happening: the 'default mode network', a set of brain regions that becomes active precisely when we are not focused on an external task. Far from being idle, this network appears to be involved in some of our most valuable mental work – making sense of past experiences, imagining the future, and forming connections between apparently unrelated ideas. Studies in which participants were given a deliberately tedious task, such as copying numbers from a phone directory, before being asked to come up with creative uses for everyday objects, have found that the bored group tended to produce more original answers. The findings are modest, and researchers are careful not to overstate them, but the direction is consistent.

3What does the writer say about the research into boredom?

Find the sentence that gives the writer's opinion of the 'findings'.

Answer C

Evidence“The findings are modest, and researchers are careful not to overstate them, but the direction is consistent.”

Why'Modest' means small or limited. 'The direction is consistent' means the studies all show the same thing. Option C says the same idea in different words.

Why not the others?

  • A 'Researchers are careful not to overstate them' – so the research does not 'prove' anything strongly.
  • B The boring task (copying numbers) is described, but nobody criticises it.
  • D The 'default mode network' is explained, not criticised.
04

This is not an argument that sits comfortably with the business model of the technology we carry around. Apps are designed, with considerable skill, to ensure that no moment goes unfilled. Every notification, every endlessly scrolling feed, is a small promise that something more interesting is just a tap away. The irony is that by removing boredom so efficiently, these tools may also be removing the conditions in which our own ideas take shape. We have, in effect, outsourced our daydreaming.

4What is the writer's main point in the fourth paragraph?

Look for the sentence that begins with 'The irony is that...'.

Answer B

Evidence“The irony is that by removing boredom so efficiently, these tools may also be removing the conditions in which our own ideas take shape.”

WhyApps fill every moment ('constant stimulation'), and this may take away the empty time we need for our own ideas to 'take shape' (develop).

Why not the others?

  • A She says apps are designed 'with considerable skill', but she never says companies hide anything.
  • C She does not tell people what they should do in this paragraph.
  • D Notifications are only one example, together with 'endlessly scrolling feeds'. She does not say they are the worst.
05

I should be careful here, because there is a version of this argument that I find rather tiresome. It usually comes from people who romanticise a pre-digital past in which everyone apparently sat in quiet contemplation, composing poetry. That world never existed. People have always found ways to distract themselves, whether through gossip, newspapers or staring out of the window at nothing in particular. And for many people, a phone is not an escape from thought but a lifeline – to family abroad, to work, to information they genuinely need.

5Why does the writer mention 'people who romanticise a pre-digital past'?

How does the writer feel about 'a version of this argument'? Does she agree with it?

Answer D

Evidence“I should be careful here, because there is a version of this argument that I find rather tiresome. ... That world never existed.”

WhyShe says some people take her argument too far, and she finds that 'tiresome'. By saying 'That world never existed', she shows she does not agree with them. Her own idea is more moderate.

Why not the others?

  • A She never says she used to think like them.
  • B She says the opposite: 'People have always found ways to distract themselves.'
  • C Refusing technology is not mentioned. She even defends phones as 'a lifeline'.
06

What I am suggesting is something less dramatic. Since my wrist recovered, I have gone back to using my phone, but I have kept a few small habits. I leave it in my bag on short journeys. I no longer fill every queue with news I will forget within minutes. I have even, on occasion, sat on a park bench doing absolutely nothing, which, I am slightly embarrassed to admit, still feels like a minor act of rebellion. The pigeons, for the record, nod to keep their vision steady. I looked it up – but only after I'd spent a pleasant ten minutes wondering.

6The writer's comment about the pigeons at the end of the text suggests that she

What adjective does she use to describe the ten minutes before she looked it up?

Answer A

Evidence“I looked it up – but only after I'd spent a pleasant ten minutes wondering.”

WhyShe calls the time spent wondering 'pleasant'. The words 'but only after' show that she chose to think first and search later. The thinking time is important to her.

Why not the others?

  • B She did use her phone: 'I looked it up'.
  • C She enjoyed the time, so it was not a problem for her.
  • D There is no regret. She chose to wait.

05

Get it right!

Look at the sentence below. It contains a common mistake. Can you correct it?

IncorrectIt was only when I lost my phone when I started to notice how often I used it.
CorrectIt was only when I lost my phone that I started to notice how often I used it.

In the pattern It was only when... that..., we use that in the second part, not 'when'. Look at the first paragraph: 'It was only when a stubborn wrist injury forced me... that I began to suspect...'
