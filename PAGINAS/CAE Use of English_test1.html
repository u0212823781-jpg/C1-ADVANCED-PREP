For questions 1–8, read the text below and decide which answer (A, B, C or D) best fits each gap. There is an example at the beginning (0).

Part 1

The four-day week

The idea of working four days instead of five has (0) gained considerable attention in recent years. Supporters argue that a shorter week does not (1) ______ lead to lower productivity. On the contrary, several large-scale trials have found that staff who are given an extra day off tend to work more efficiently and (2) ______ fewer days off sick. Employers taking part in these trials have also reported that it is easier to (3) ______ talented workers, who are less likely to leave for a competitor. However, critics point out that the model is far from suitable for every (4) ______ of business. Hospitals, schools and shops cannot simply close for an extra day, and in these sectors the change may (5) ______ to little more than longer shifts rather than genuine free time. There are also concerns that some staff may feel under (6) ______ to squeeze five days of work into four, which could lead to higher levels of stress. Whether the four-day week will (7) ______ on in the long term is still uncertain. What seems clear, however, is that the traditional nine-to-five week can no longer be (8) ______ for granted.

1

AnswerB – necessarily

We say something does not necessarily happen = it does not always happen. The other words do not go with 'not ... lead to'.

2

AnswerC – take

The fixed phrase is take days off (sick). We never say 'make' or 'do' days off.

3

AnswerD – retain

To retain workers means to keep them in the company. 'Remain' has no object, and 'sustain' and 'contain' have different meanings.

4

AnswerA – line

Line of business means a type of business or work. 'Field' is close, but we say 'field of work', not 'field of business'.

5

AnswerC – amount

Amount to means 'be the same as in the end'. 'Equal' has no 'to', and 'add' needs 'up to'.

6

AnswerB – pressure

The phrase is feel under pressure to do something. The other nouns do not go with 'under ... to'.

7

AnswerD – catch

Catch on means 'become popular'. 'Take on', 'get on' and 'pick on' have completely different meanings.

8

AnswerB – taken

The fixed phrase is take something for granted = believe it will always be true or always exist.

For questions 9–16, read the text below and think of the word which best fits each gap. Use only one word in each gap. There is an example at the beginning (0).

Part 2

Why songs get stuck in our heads

Most of us have had the experience of a tune getting stuck in (0) our head for hours, 9 matter how hard we try to forget it. Psychologists refer to this as an 'earworm', and research suggests that 10 than ninety percent of people experience one at least once a week. Songs are particularly likely to stick if they are simple, repetitive and have been heard recently, 11 is why advertisers are so fond of catchy jingles. Interestingly, the tune is rarely remembered in full. 12, we tend to replay the same short section over and over again, as 13 our brain were trying to complete an unfinished task. Some researchers believe that earworms are most 14 to occur when the mind is not fully occupied, 15 as when we are walking or doing housework. The good news is that there appears to be a simple cure: chewing gum. Studies have shown that it makes 16 harder for the brain to 'hear' the song, because the movement of the jaw interferes with our inner voice.

Answer9. no

No matter how + adjective/adverb means 'it is not important how much'.

Answer10. more

More than + number means 'over'. The word 'than' after the gap is the clue.

Answer11. which

Which is why refers back to the whole idea before the comma. 'That' is not possible after a comma.

Answer12. instead

Instead shows the opposite of the sentence before: we don't remember the whole song; instead, we replay a small part.

Answer13. if / though

As if / as though + past form ('were') compares with something that is not really true.

Answer14. likely

Be (most) likely to + verb means 'probably happen'. 'Most' before the gap is the clue.

Answer15. such

Such as introduces examples: walking or doing housework.

Answer16. it

Make it harder for someone to do something. 'It' is needed as the object, because the real object comes later ('to hear the song').

For questions 17–24, read the text below. Use the word given in capitals at the end of some of the lines to form a word that fits in the gap in the same line. There is an example at the beginning (0).

Part 3

Science for everyone

Citizen science projects invite members of the public to make a real (0) contribution CONTRIBUTE to research. Thanks to smartphones, taking part has never been more 17ACCESS. Volunteers may count birds, classify galaxies or record the 18APPEAR of the first flowers in spring. In the past, many scientists questioned the 19RELY of data collected by amateurs, and early results were often 20COMPLETE. However, recent studies have shown that, with proper training, volunteers can be 21SURPRISE accurate. Such projects also help to raise public 22AWARE of environmental issues, and many participants describe the experience as deeply 23REWARD. The main challenge for researchers is to keep volunteers 24MOTIVE over long periods, since interest tends to fade once the novelty wears off.

Answer17. accessible

After 'more' we need an adjective: more accessible = easier to use or join.

Answer18. appearance

After 'the' we need a noun: the appearance of = the moment something appears.

Answer19. reliability

After 'the' and before 'of' we need a noun: the reliability of data = how much we can trust it.

Answer20. incomplete

'Were often' needs an adjective. The meaning is negative (scientists questioned the data), so we add a prefix: incomplete.

Answer21. surprisingly

Before the adjective 'accurate' we need an adverb: surprisingly accurate.

Answer22. awareness

Raise awareness of is a fixed phrase. After 'public' we need a noun.

Answer23. rewarding

After 'deeply' we need an adjective that describes the experience: rewarding = it gives you satisfaction.

Answer24. motivated

Keep someone + adjective. The volunteers feel the motivation, so we use the -ed adjective: motivated.

For questions 25–30, complete the second sentence so that it has a similar meaning to the first sentence, using the word given. Do not change the word given. You must use between three and six words, including the word given.

Part 4
Remember: write between three and six words, including the word given. Do not change the word given. Contractions count as two words (can't = can not).
Example: James would only speak to the head of department. ON → James insisted on speaking to the head of department.

25I didn't realise how difficult the exam would be until I started studying for it.

ONLY

It was studying for it that I realised how difficult the exam would be.

0 words

Answeronly when I started / only after I started / only once I started

It was only when ... that ... is a way to give emphasis to the moment something happened. We need 'when' + past simple: 'only when I started'.

26People say that the castle was built in the twelfth century.

SAID

The castle in the twelfth century.

0 words

Answeris said to have been built

is said to have been built: 'is said to' + 'have been' + past participle, because the building happened in the past and the castle did not build itself (passive).

SHOULD

You my car without asking.

0 words

Answershould not have borrowed / shouldn't have borrowed

We use should not have + past participle to criticise something that somebody did in the past.

Answerbeing very upset, Sarah went / being very upset, she went / being so upset, Sarah went / being so upset, she went / her being very upset, she went

After despite we use a noun or verb + -ing ('being upset'). Go on with something means 'continue with' it.

He the meeting.

Answercan't have been aware of / cannot have been aware of / couldn't have been aware of / could not have been aware of

Can't have + past participle means 'I'm sure it was not true' in the past. 'Know about' = be aware of.
